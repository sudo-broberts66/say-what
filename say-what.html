A small word, slowed down

Why do we say What? when we already heard it?

Because your mouth has a deadline and your understanding doesn’t. In conversation you get about a fifth of a second to respond. When the meaning hasn’t arrived by then, your brain sends out the one word that needs no planning, just to hold your place. The sentence is still ringing in a short-lived memory of sound, and you finish decoding it a moment after what? has already left.

You can check each part of that on this page, starting with your own reflexes.

First, measure yourself

You answer faster than you can react

Tap the panel, wait, and tap again the instant it changes colour. This is the simplest thing a nervous system can do on cue: see a change, move a finger.

Across languages, the most common pause between one speaker stopping and the next starting is around 200 milliseconds. That is about as fast as a person can react to anything, and replying takes far more work than tapping.

The sums don’t work unless you start early. Planning even one word takes about 600 ms, so to speak 200 ms after someone stops, you must begin building your reply while they are still talking. You are guessing how their sentence ends and what it will mean, and getting your answer ready on that guess.

You do this dozens of times a minute without noticing. What? is what it sounds like when the guess comes due and there is nothing behind it yet.

What happens

The second track is the part most people have never been told about. Your hearing keeps a raw copy of the last few seconds of sound, called echoic memory, whether or not you were paying attention. It’s why you can “replay” the last thing someone said after they complain you weren’t listening.

So the sentence was never lost. It was waiting, undecoded, when your turn arrived. After roughly half a second of silence the pause itself starts to say something, and your brain will not let that happen. It fires the cheapest word it owns. The decoding keeps running on the echo, finishes a few hundred milliseconds later, and you answer a question you just claimed not to have heard.

Requests like this do tend to come late. In recorded conversation they most often start about 700 ms after the other person stops, three times the usual gap. That extra time is you, trying to work it out alone first.

Same word, three jobs

It was never asking for a thing

In none of the three does “what” stand for an object you’re missing. English took its emptiest question word and gave it a job that has little to do with questions: handing the turn back to the other person while you catch up, check, or get ready.

It isn’t just English

Every language tested built the same tool

English has a second, even smaller version: huh? In 2013 a team of linguists went looking for it elsewhere, in recordings of everyday talk from five continents. Tap one.

Unrelated languages, with no shared ancestor for the word, arrived at nearly the same sound: one syllable, an open vowel close to where the mouth sits at rest, the pitch of a question. The researchers’ explanation is convergent evolution, the way sharks and dolphins ended up the same shape. Every community of speakers faces the same half-second problem, and the fastest possible syllable is the solution each of them found.

So a what? that comes out just before you understand is your brain keeping a promise it made while the other person was still speaking. It buys a third of a second. That is usually enough.
