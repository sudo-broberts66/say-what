An answer you step inside

Why do we say What? when we already heard it?

Because your mouth has a deadline and your understanding doesn’t. The next few minutes put you inside the half-second where one outruns the other.

Scene 3 · The deadline

You answer faster than you can react

Why was your mouth in such a hurry? Tap the panel, wait, and tap again the instant it changes colour.

The gap between two people’s turns≈ 200 ms
A typical reaction to a colour change≈ 250 ms
Getting a single word ready to say≈ 600 ms

People start replying about 200 ms after the other person stops. That is as fast as a human can react to anything, and a single word takes three times longer to prepare. So you begin building your reply while they are still talking, on a guess about how their sentence ends. What? is the sound of that guess coming due with nothing behind it.

Scene 4 · The chorus

Every language tested built the same tool

English has a smaller version, huh? In 2013 linguists went looking for it in recordings of everyday talk from five continents. Tap one to hear its shape.

Unrelated languages arrived at nearly the same sound: one syllable, an open vowel close to where the mouth sits at rest, the pitch of a question. The researchers call it convergent evolution, the way sharks and dolphins ended up the same shape. Every community faces the same half-second problem, and this is the fastest syllable a mouth can make.

Scene 5 · One word, three melodies

It was never asking for a thing

In none of the three does “what” stand for an object you’re missing. English took its emptiest question word and gave it a different job: handing the turn back while you catch up, check, or get ready.

Scene 6 · Back at the table

A what? that comes out just before you understand is your brain keeping a promise it made while the other person was still speaking. It buys a third of a second. That is usually enough.

Where the numbers come from
  1. Turn gaps cluster near 200 ms across ten languages: Stivers et al., “Universals and cultural variation in turn-taking in conversation”, PNAS, 2009.
  2. Planning a word takes about 600 ms, so replies are prepared in overlap: Levinson & Torreira, “Timing in turn-taking and its implications for processing models of language”, Frontiers in Psychology, 2015.
  3. Requests for repetition most often begin after about 700 ms: Kendrick, “The intersection of turn-taking and repair”, Frontiers in Psychology, 2015.
  4. An auditory store lasting a few seconds: Darwin, Turvey & Crowder, “An auditory analogue of the Sperling partial report procedure”, Cognitive Psychology, 1972.
  5. The same word everywhere: Dingemanse, Torreira & Enfield, “Is ‘Huh?’ a universal word?”, PLOS ONE, 2013. Spellings are approximate.
  6. One repair about every 1.4 minutes: Dingemanse et al., “Universal principles in the repair of communication problems”, PLOS ONE, 2015.

The fixed marks (200 ms, 600 ms, 700 ms, the echo’s few seconds) are measured averages. How the sliders move the moment of understanding is a simplified model, and the dinner scene is staged with it. The sounds are a rough formant synthesizer: they carry the pitch and vowel of each word, not a real speaker. Touchscreens add a few tens of milliseconds to the reaction test.
